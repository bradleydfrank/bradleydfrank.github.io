How it works
Fitting

Each set of three clicks defines exactly one circle (the circumcircle). The first triplet is the outer droplet boundary, radius R_drop; the second is the internal interface between the two phases, radius R_inner. Everything else follows from those two circles and the distance d between their centres.

Upper and lower phases

Results are reported for the upper and lower side of the droplet, named for where they sit in the image rather than for what they are made of. The tool assumes the droplet is aligned with gravity and imaged from the side, so the denser phase settles below the lighter one and the symmetry axis runs vertically in the frame. That assumption is what makes a purely geometric measurement possible, and it holds for the usual density-separated case.[1]

So the upper phase might be a hydrocarbon or a silicone, and the lower one a fluorocarbon — but equally a polymer, or any other denser phase. Nothing in the calculation depends on the chemistry; only on which side is which.

Which side is which is decided by which centre sits lower in the image, as in the original MATLAB app. Use Flip if your image is upside down relative to the physical setup. When the centre axis is far from vertical that test rests on only a few pixels, so the tool warns and suggests rotating.

Working through a field

Press Enter to accept the droplet you have just fitted and start the next one straight away. Accepted droplets stay drawn on the image, numbered to match the table: the droplet outline and the interface line only, since the rest of the inner circle is construction geometry.

Only one droplet shows its readout at a time — the one you are placing, or the one selected. Click a table row (or the number on the image, or press ↑/↓) to select a droplet; the view scrolls to it if it is off-screen. Each droplet's centre position is saved in the CSV, so every row can be traced back to a place on the image.

Spherical Janus mode

The contact angle comes from the law of cosines on the triangle (R_drop, R_inner, d). The internal interface splits the sphere into a lens and a remainder; their volumes are exact spherical-cap volumes. The Janus ratio is the ratio of the two exposed surface areas. This is the morphology assumed by the classic balance of interfacial tensions at the three-phase contact line.[1]

Refractive-index correction

The internal interface is viewed through the curved outer surface of the droplet, so its apparent position and curvature are distorted by refraction. Correcting for this was introduced by Nagelberg et al.[1], who de-warped each point of the apparent interface (in the paraxial approximation) before fitting a circle to it.

This tool uses the cruder version carried over from the MATLAB app: a single factor n_cont / n_phase applied to R_inner and d after the circle is fitted. It is the same idea to first order, but it is not equivalent to the point-by-point correction, so treat it as an approximation. The original also applied that factor to the numerator of the law of cosines but not the denominator; that expression is not the law of cosines for any triangle when the factor differs from 1, and it can drive the cosine outside ±1. The checkbox above switches to the consistent form. With all three indices equal — the default — the two are identical, so existing measurements are unaffected.

Non-spherical Janus mode

For droplets that are not a single sphere — two partially fused lobes. The two triplets are simply the two lobes. Reported are the angle between the interfaces at the neck, the exposed area of each lobe, the neck radius, and each lobe's volume (sphere minus the cap past the contact plane).
